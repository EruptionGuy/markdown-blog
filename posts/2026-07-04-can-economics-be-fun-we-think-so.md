When people hear the word economics, they often picture graphs, formulas, and long lectures. It is a subject that many students see as difficult or even intimidating.

But does it have to be?

Last weekend, eCON had the opportunity to visit [SOS Children's Village Hải Phòng](https://sosvietnam.org/villages/hai-phong), where we organized a STEM Fair and a financial literacy workshop for the children. We arrived hoping to make economics more accessible through interactive activities, simple games, and open conversation. By the end of the day, we left with something even more valuable: a reminder that the best way to learn economics is not always through a textbook, but through curiosity, discussion, and real-life examples.

# Morning Game: Guess the Price

One of the most popular activities at the STEM Fair was Guess the Price. The rules were simple. We showed the children pictures of everyday items and asked them to estimate how much they cost. At first glance, it seemed like a lighthearted guessing game. In reality, it became a surprisingly thoughtful exercise in observation and reasoning.

What happened next surprised us.

Instead of making random guesses, many of the children began asking questions. Were these prices from Hà Nội or Hải Phòng? Had inflation already been taken into account? Were the items brand-name products or more common alternatives? Their answers were often remarkably close, but what impressed us even more was the way they approached the problem. They were not just guessing. They were comparing, analyzing, and using logic to support their estimates.

Without realizing it, they were already thinking like economists. In fact, they were thinking more carefully than we had expected, because our initial game design did not fully account for external factors such as location, inflation, or differences in everyday spending habits. We had planned for simple guesses, but the children naturally brought those variables into the conversation, showing us that economic thinking often begins with noticing the details we overlook. A price is never just a number. It reflects where something is sold, how much people are willing to pay, and how the market around it changes over time.

# Afternoon Lesson: Needs vs. Wants

The conversations continued during our afternoon session on Needs and Wants. Rather than treating it as a lesson with right or wrong answers, we encouraged the children to explain their reasoning and challenge one another respectfully. That approach made the discussion much more lively, and it quickly became clear that the children were not afraid to think beyond the examples we had prepared.

One discussion quickly stood out.

When we classified an iPad as a Want, one child confidently argued that it should actually be considered a Need. Their point reminded us that the line between needs and wants is not always fixed. A tablet can be a want when it is used for games, videos, or entertainment, but it can also become a need when a student uses it to attend online classes, read digital textbooks, complete assignments, or search for information. The same idea applies to many other things. A smartphone may seem like a luxury, but for a child who needs to contact family or access school materials, it can be essential. A pair of shoes can be a want when someone is choosing a stylish design, but it becomes a need when it is necessary for walking to school every day. Even something as simple as internet access can be both a convenience and a necessity, depending on how much a person relies on it for learning and communication. In today's 4.0 era, technology has become such an important part of education that an iPad can feel less like a luxury and more like a necessity.

Whether or not everyone agreed was never the point.

What mattered was that the children were questioning assumptions, defending their ideas, and thinking critically about how economic concepts apply to everyday life. That single conversation reminded us that economics is not about memorizing definitions. It is about understanding how people make choices in a changing world, and recognizing that those choices are shaped by context, access, and need.

Our visit also included tours of Tân Vũ Port and Nam Đình Vũ Port, two important parts of Hải Phòng's port system and some of the busiest logistics gateways in northern Vietnam. Hải Phòng is often called the country's main seaport in the North, and these ports help connect factories, warehouses, and international shipping routes. What made the visit especially interesting was seeing how quickly cargo moves from ship to shore to truck, with containers arriving from across the region and heading out to industrial zones, distribution centers, and export markets. It was a vivid reminder that behind every product we use, there is a whole network of trade, transport, planning, and coordination working quietly in the background. The economic ideas we discussed with the children in the morning suddenly felt much larger and more tangible when we saw how goods actually move through the real economy.

![port](images/blog-images/2026-07-04-can-economics-be-fun-we-think-so/port.JPG)

Looking back, the most memorable part of the trip was not a perfect answer or a successful activity. It was the curiosity the children brought to every conversation. Their questions reminded us that learning becomes far more meaningful when students are encouraged to explore, debate, and discover ideas for themselves. Sometimes, the most powerful lessons come not from being told the answer, but from being invited to think through the question.

At eCON, we believe economics should inspire curiosity, not fear. Sometimes, all it takes is a simple game, an unexpected question, or a different perspective to show that economics is not just something we study. It is something we live every day.
