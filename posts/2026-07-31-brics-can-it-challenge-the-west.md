Originally coined in 2001 by Goldman Sachs economist Jim O’Neill as an investment acronym, BRIC evolved into a formal geopolitical bloc in 2009\. What began as a dialogue between major emerging market economies has transformed into a focal point for the Global South's drive toward a multipolar world order. With its recent expansion, BRICS has significantly increased its economic weight, triggering debate over whether it can effectively challenge the institutional dominance long held by Western powers and the Group of Seven (G7).

# Member Evolution: From BRICS to BRICS+

The coalition's membership expanded significantly across South America, Eurasia, Africa, and the Middle East over two key phases.

The original core comprised Brazil, Russia, India, and China, which held their first formal summit in 2009\. South Africa joined shortly after in 2010, completing the initial BRICS lineup.

In January 2024, the bloc initiated a major expansion by formally integrating Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) as full members. Saudi Arabia actively participated in expansion proceedings and integrated into bloc events, while Indonesia formally joined as a full member in early 2025\. To manage growing interest from other nations without requiring full membership, the coalition also introduced a designated Partner Country status to deepen institutional ties across Latin America, Asia, and Eastern Europe.

# Core Strengths: Economic and Strategic Muscle

The expanded BRICS+ coalition commands substantial economic leverage on the global stage. Representing roughly 45% of the world’s population, the bloc possesses an enormous consumer base and workforce that drives deep internal market potential independent of Western demand.

In terms of global resources, the inclusion of major Middle Eastern energy producers alongside Russia and Brazil places over 40% of global crude oil production under member control, alongside massive shares of natural gas, agricultural commodities, and critical industrial metals.

When measured by Purchasing Power Parity (PPP), the combined economic output of BRICS+ accounts for over 35% of global GDP, surpassing the G7's roughly 30% share. To convert this output into financial independence, the Shanghai-based New Development Bank (NDB) provides infrastructure funding without the strict political conditions attached to traditional IMF or World Bank loans, while member states increasingly settle intra-group trade in local currencies to bypass the U.S. dollar.

# Strategic Weaknesses: Internal Fractures and Institutional Gaps

Despite its collective scale, BRICS+ faces severe structural limitations that prevent it from operating as a seamless geopolitical bloc.

Unlike the G7, which shares core democratic values and security alignments, BRICS+ spans vastly different political systems and strategic interests. Long-standing border rivalries between China and India, alongside complex regional friction in the Middle East, make reaching unanimous political positions difficult.

Economic asymmetry also presents a hurdle. China accounts for more than 60% of the total GDP of the grouping, raising concerns among smaller members about swapping Western influence for economic reliance on Beijing.

Furthermore, establishing a unified "BRICS currency" remains functionally impractical due to stark differences in monetary policies, capital controls, and inflation rates across member countries. Because BRICS functions primarily as an informal consultative forum rather than a binding trade union or military alliance, decision-making can be slow and lacks centralized enforcement mechanisms.

# Impact on the G7 and the Global Order

| Indicator | BRICS+ | G7 |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Share of Global Population** | \~45% | \~10% |
| **Share of Global GDP (PPP)** | \~36% | \~31% |
| **Global Crude Oil Production** | \>40% | \~15% |
| **Core Cohesion** | Low to Moderate (Diverse political aims) | High (Shared security & economic values) |

Rather than dismantling Western institutions, BRICS+ functions primarily as a platform for emerging economies to hedge their options in an increasingly divided world. By building alternative supply lines, insulating themselves from unilateral sanctions, and demanding greater representation in international bodies like the UN and World Bank, member nations are actively reshaping global trade. While internal divisions will likely prevent BRICS+ from functioning as a single global superpower, its growth ensures that the Global South will exercise far greater leverage over the future of the global economy.
