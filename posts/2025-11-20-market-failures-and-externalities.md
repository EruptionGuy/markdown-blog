# What is a Market Failure?

According to perfect competition, resources like goods and services are supposed to be allocated efficiently. In reality, however, resources are not maximized all the time, leading to an outcome known as market failure.

Market failure is a situation where a free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, which means that resources are not utilized to the maximum. In situations such as monopolies, public good or most commonly externalities, market failure can be observed.

# What are Externalities?

Externalities can be understood as the effects of an activity that are not borne directly by that economic activity. The effects include both benefits and cost which can be explained by positive externalities and negative externalities. Externalities create market failures because there is a disparity between the costs/benefits from producers or consumers (private) and the costs/benefits from society (social)

# Positive vs. Negative Externalities

How positive and negative externalities are presented in reality can be clearly illustrated by the difference in concepts of education and pollution.

## Education as a Positive Externality

Upon receiving education, an individual can later contribute to society by increasing productivity and innovations, which are advantages that bring benefits to society as a whole. However, not everyone will be willing to pursue education; these individuals may only focus on personal benefits, thus the education sector might be underconsumed.

## Pollution as a Negative Externality

When producing goods, factories will emit greenhouse gases into the air or discharge waste into waters, causing significant damage to the environment and the population’s health. Despite the fact that these organizations pay for materials and labor, the social costs still exceed the private costs.

# Government Interventions (Taxes, Subsidies, Regulation) 

To ameliorate the effects of negative externalities, the government can impose taxes on these activities to discourage companies from harmful production and adopt a more sustainable practice instead. For example, the government can implement carbon taxes to reduce the emission of carbon dioxide. In contrast, activities that generate positive externalities can be promoted by providing subsidies or support. Practices such as funding schools, free healthcare, encouraging participation, and even long-term investments. When demanded, regulations are necessary to ensure policies are enacted strictly; for instance, a ban on harmful substances.
