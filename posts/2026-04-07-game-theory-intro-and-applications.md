# Nash Equilibrium

Nash Equilibrium refers to a game theory concept, a theoretical framework for analyzing social situations among non-cooperating players to determine the most optimal action to secure the best outcomes, where the game reaches a state in which players have no incentives to deviate from their current strategy. This decision stems from the fact that players assume they know (or at least strongly assume they do) their opponent’s strategy and cannot improve by changing their strategy as it remains the most optimal.

Therefore, if all players use their optimal strategies, an individual won’t receive any benefits from changing strategies, assuming that the others remain constant in their strategies. A game may have one, multiple, or no Nash equilibria.

# Prisoner’s Dilemma

The prisoner’s dilemma is the most famous example used to explain Nash Equilibrium. In the classic scenario, there are two prisoners (David and John) who are interrogated in separate rooms and cannot communicate. However, both have the option to either remain silent (cooperate with the other) or confess to the crime (betray).

## Payoff Table

|  | John: Silent | John: Confess |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **David: Silent** | Both get 1 year | David: 5 years, John: free |
| **David: Confess** | David: free, John: 5 years | Both get 3 years |


If both prisoners remain silent, they both get a 1-year sentence. If both prisoners confess, they both get 3 years in jail each. Lastly, if David confesses while John stays silent, David is free to go while John gets a 5-year prison sentence and vice versa. 

It is obvious that the dilemma is structured such that both choosing to remain silent achieves a more advantageous joint result. However, such a decision to remain silent has a higher degree of risk than confessing. Regardless of what the other person does, betraying will always be the most optimal individual choice.

If the other chooses to stay silent, you go free; if the other chooses to betray, you serve 3 years instead of 5\. This is why in the most rational situation, both will choose to confess for their best individual interest. That situation is the Nash Equilibrium in this game.

# Applications in Business Competition

## Price Wars and Retail Competition

* Consider two competing retail stores (e.g., Target and Walmart).  
* If one raises prices to increase profit, the other can keep prices low and attract customers.  
* If both lower prices, profits fall for both.

Therefore, they will be “stuck” at low prices as neither can trust the other one to raise their prices first. This is the Nash Equilibrium of low prices.

## Advertising and Market Spending

* Picture two competing companies selling similar products (e.g., Coca-Cola and Pepsi).  
* If neither advertises, both save costs.  
* However, if one advertises while the other does not, the advertiser gains market share.

Hence, both companies are “forced” to spend large amounts on advertising. The Nash Equilibrium here is the state where both have to spend heavily on advertisements, or it will lead to a competitive disadvantage.

## Product Feature Mimicry 

* Picture two big technology companies in the smartphone industry (Apple and Samsung).  
* If Apple decides to introduce a new feature (e.g., a triple-lens camera), Samsung is incentivized to do the same immediately.

If Samsung doesn’t do so, it will risk losing market share. If Samsung does, it maintains its market share but incurs higher research and development costs.

Consequently, competitors will sell nearly identical features in standardized products at similar price points, as any deviation will give the other an advantage.
