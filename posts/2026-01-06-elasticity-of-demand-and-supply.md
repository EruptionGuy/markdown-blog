Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) refers to how sensitive consumers are to changes in price. In simple terms, it shows how the quantity demanded of a product changes when its price changes, while everything else stays the same.

Another way to understand this is that price elasticity of demand measures the change in quantity demanded compared to the change in price. However, a product is considered elastic if a small change in price leads to a large change in demand. On the other hand, if demand changes very little despite price fluctuations, it is said to be inelastic.

This concept is important because it helps businesses and policymakers predict consumer behavior, set prices more effectively, and understand how demand responds to price changes.

An analogous concept to PED is the Price Elasticity of Supply (PES). In this case, PES measures how responsive the quantity supplied of a good or service is relative to a change in price. The crucial difference is that price elasticity of supply is focused on the supply side rather than the demand side.

# Price Elasticity Formula

![image1](images/blog-images/2026-01-06-elasticity-of-demand-and-supply/image1.jpg)
<center>The Formulas of Demand and Supply</center>

To understand elasticity more easily, we need to look at the formulas used to calculate it. Both Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) and Price Elasticity of Supply (PES) measure how responsive volume is to a change in price.   
   
For Price Elasticity of Demand, the formula compares:

* Percentage change in quantity demanded, which shows how much consumers increase or decrease their purchases. This is calculated by taking the change in quantity (new − old) divided by the old quantity, then multiplied by 100%.  
* Percentage change in price, calculated in the same way using the new and old prices.

   
So, the PED formula measures how important demand changes when price changes.   
   
An important thing to remember is that demand curves slope downward, meaning price and quantity move in opposite directions. Because of this, PED values are technically negative. However, in economics, we generally ignore the negative sign and use the absolute value, so elasticity is always expressed as a positive number.

The formula for Price Elasticity of Supply is nearly identical to PED. Just like demand, supply can also be classified as elastic or inelastic.

* If the value is greater than 1, supply is elastic, meaning producers can easily increase or decrease output when prices change.  
* If the value is less than 1, supply is inelastic, meaning the product does not respond much to price changes.

   
Goods with elastic supply are generally easier and quicker to produce, while inelastic ones are common for products that take a long time to make or depend on limited resources.

# Factors Affecting Elasticity 

There are a number of factors that determine how elastic or inelastic demand and supply are. One of the most important factors affecting the elasticity of demand is the presence of substitutes. The demand for goods that have close substitutes, like branded clothing or soft drinks, tends to be more elastic since it is easier to switch to alternatives when prices increase.

Another important determinant is whether the good is a necessity or a luxury. Necessities, such as food staples or electricity, tend to have inelastic demand since consumers must have them regardless of price changes. Luxury goods, on the other hand, tend to have elastic demand because consumers can cut back on consumption if prices rise.

Another important factor affecting elasticity is time. Demand and supply are usually inelastic in the shorter run because consumers and producers do not have much scope for adjusting their behaviour. Over time, when they find alternatives, and firms adjust the level of production, elasticity increases.

For supply, the two important determinants are production flexibility and resource availability. Those commodities whose production is swift and inexpensive tend to have an elastic supply, whereas commodities with lengthy production periods or whose production requires rare resources have inelastic supply.

# Elastic vs. Inelastic Examples

Products that exhibit elasticity are those in which a slight variation in price results in a significant shift in the quantity supplied or demanded. Fast fashion items and restaurant meals, for instance, have elastic demand because consumers can readily decide not to purchase them or switch to other options when prices increase. Starbucks coffee is an illustration of elastic demand. There are numerous close substitutes for Starbucks products, including neighborhood cafés, other international chains, and homemade coffee. Customers can simply cut back on their consumption or switch to less expensive options when Starbucks raises its prices. Because of this, a slight price increase may result in a comparatively large drop in the quantity demanded, demonstrating the elasticity of demand for Starbucks coffee.

Conversely, when prices fluctuate, inelastic goods exhibit minimal changes in quantity. Basic foods, fuel, and necessary medications are typical examples. Demand is largely stable because consumers still need to buy these products even if prices rise. One example of inelastic demand is insulin manufactured by pharmaceutical firms like Novo Nordisk or Eli Lilly. Patients with diabetes need insulin to survive, and there are not many alternatives. Patients must keep buying insulin in order to survive, even if its cost rises dramatically. Insulin is therefore very inelastic, meaning that changes in price have little impact on the quantity demanded.
